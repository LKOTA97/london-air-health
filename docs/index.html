How to read this

This is an ecological comparison of areas, not people. Inner London boroughs are both denser and, in many cases, more deprived, and they sit where traffic and the urban background are highest, so deprivation and pollution are tangled with geography.

The thirds come from sql/03_deprivation_groups.sql, which splits the 32 boroughs with NTILE(3) on the IMD 2025 score. IMD 2025 is a relative measure: it ranks areas but does not say how much more deprived one is than another, and it is not directly comparable with IMD 2019.

These charts compare areas. They do not show that PM2.5 causes admissions: smoking, deprivation, housing, access to primary care and how hospitals code admissions all differ between boroughs. The admission periods are financial years, while PM2.5 is a calendar year. The model on the next page counts deaths only, not admissions.

Why the horizon matters

The model gives one present value: the most London could spend, in today's money, for the QALYs gained. Spreading that same sum evenly over 10 years gives a larger yearly figure than spreading it over 30. At £20,000 per QALY, London's break-even spend is £198.8m a year over 10 years, £116.3m over 20 and £89.9m over 30. This choice moves the answer by more than a factor of two, so the tables show all three horizons. That reading assumes the years of spending buy a cut that then lasts, as with new infrastructure.

If instead the cut lasts only while the money is spent and the benefit then fades on the same lag, the break-even spend barely depends on the horizon: £64.1m, £68.2m and £70.5m a year over 10, 20 and 30 years at £20,000 per QALY. Use this view for schemes that need running costs every year.

Thresholds: NICE guidelines, which cover public health, state £20,000 per QALY. £25,000 to £35,000 has applied to NICE technology appraisals of medicines since spring 2026 and is shown for comparison only. The societal value (£84,548 per QALY) comes from Defra's air quality appraisal guidance and is a cost-benefit value, not the NHS's opportunity cost, so it uses Green Book discounting as revised on 28 October 2026: 3.0% a year for spending and health, falling after year 30, with health values uprated by 2.0% a year.

Method in plain English

Question. What is a permanent cut of 1 µg/m³ in annual mean PM2.5 worth in each London borough, in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs)? How much could be spent each year to achieve it and stay cost-effective? In practice much of London’s PM2.5 is regional, so read each borough’s figure as the value to its residents of a London-wide cut. Under COMEAP’s linear, no-threshold relationship a 1 µg/m³ cut is worth the same per person whatever the starting level, so borough figures differ by population, not by how polluted the borough is.

Effect on death rates. Long-term exposure to PM2.5 raises the risk of death from all causes. COMEAP (2022) recommends a hazard ratio of 1.08 per 10 µg/m³ (95% interval 1.06 to 1.09), linear with no cut-off. A 1 µg/m³ cut multiplies the death rate at each age by 0.9923 once fully in effect. The same coefficient sits behind the official Fingertips figures, and a test in this project checks that.

Delay. The benefit builds up over time, following the US EPA cessation lag that COMEAP recommends: 30% in the first year, 50% more over years 2 to 5 and the last 20% over years 6 to 20.

Who gains. Residents aged 30 and over in mid-2025, by sex and single year of age, followed to age 100 using England’s life table (2022-2024). This closed cohort leaves out people who turn 30 later, so it is conservative; the open cohort is a scenario. Boroughs differ by population size and age structure; a scenario scales death rates by each borough’s standardised mortality ratio.

From life-years to money. Each extra year of life is weighted by the average EQ-5D-5L score for that age and sex to give QALYs (NICE’s preferred value set since August 2026), and discounted at 3.5% a year (NICE). The break-even present value is discounted QALYs times a cost-effectiveness threshold. Spread evenly over 20 years, that gives the break-even annual spend. Costs and health are both discounted from the end of year 1, so the annual figure does not depend on that convention. Death rates are held at 2022-2024 levels throughout (period, not projected, mortality): no future fall in death rates is assumed.

Checks. The model’s life expectancy at 30 is within 0.03 years of ONS for both sexes. Tests confirm that a hazard ratio of 1 gives no gain, a 2 µg/m³ cut gives about twice the gain, and the London total equals the sum of the boroughs.

Limitations
  • Deaths only, so likely conservative overall. Illness, children’s health and NHS savings are left out. Public Health England (2018) estimated NHS and social care savings from a 1 µg/m³ cut, but its scenario is described inconsistently (one year or sustained), so it is not added. Against that, extra years are valued at average population quality of life, which may be too high for the people with heart and lung disease whose deaths are delayed.
  • No moving. Residents are followed for life as if they never leave their borough. London has high migration, so borough figures are best read as the value to current residents of a London-wide cut.
  • Who is counted. The base case counts current residents aged 30 and over; COMEAP’s own method follows the whole current population. The open-cohort scenario adds people who turn 30 in the next 19 years but not births or newcomers.
  • Whose opportunity cost. NICE thresholds reflect what the NHS gives up elsewhere. A council spending its own public health grant faces a different trade-off; the societal value shows a wider view.
  • Linear effect. The value per µg/m³ is roughly constant for small cuts. Do not scale it up to large cuts without re-running the model.
  • England death rates in the base case: inner and outer boroughs differ only in size and age structure unless the SMR scenario is used.
  • Modelled exposure. PM2.5 comes from Defra’s model at 1 km resolution, weighted by population. Exposure for any one person differs.
  • Utilities are England-wide norms from the Health Survey for England 2017 and 2018, with no London-specific values.
  • The PSA varies the hazard ratio only, so its 95% interval reflects that uncertainty alone. The one-way analysis covers the structural choices.
  • Perspective. The break-even refers to public spending and leaves out any NHS and social care savings, which would raise it.
